VRF vs Ducted vs Cassette vs Split AC: Which System Is Right for You?

VRF, ducted, cassette and split are the four system types behind almost every air-conditioned space in Bengaluru, yet most buyers only ever compare two of them before deciding, usually on price alone. Understanding how each actually works, and where each genuinely excels, is what prevents an expensive mismatch between the system you buy and the space you're trying to cool.

Quick answer. Split suits a single room, cassette suits a mid-size room needing even airflow from the ceiling, ducted suits an open floor or cluster of rooms needing concealed, uniform cooling, and VRF suits a large building with many zones that each need independent control from a shared, efficient outdoor plant.

How each system actually works

All four system types move heat from inside a space to outside using the same basic refrigeration cycle, refrigerant absorbs heat indoors, is compressed, releases that heat outdoors, and returns to absorb more. What differentiates them is how the indoor unit is configured, how many zones one outdoor unit can serve, and how independently each zone can be controlled. Those three factors, configuration, zone count, and control independence, are really what you are choosing between, far more than any single spec-sheet number.

A useful way to think about the four options is as a spectrum: split sits at the simple, single-zone end; VRF sits at the complex, many-zone end; cassette and ducted occupy the middle, each suited to a different room shape and floor layout. None of the four is universally "better", each is the right answer for a particular kind of space.

Split AC: simple, direct, room-by-room

A split system pairs one outdoor condenser with one indoor wall-mounted unit, connected by refrigerant piping. It is the most familiar configuration to most people because it is exactly what is installed in homes across the country. For offices, splits work best for a standalone cabin, a small meeting room, or a single-person office where independent control of that one room is more important than a unified building-wide system.

The advantages are speed of installation, low upfront cost, and simple servicing, almost any technician can diagnose and repair a split unit. The trade-off is scale: wiring a large floor with many individual splits means many outdoor units cluttering a facade or rooftop, inconsistent efficiency across units of different ages, and no shared control layer unless you add one separately.

Cassette AC: even airflow from the ceiling

A cassette unit mounts flush into a false ceiling and discharges conditioned air in multiple directions, typically four-way, giving more even coverage across a room than a single wall-mounted split, particularly in squarer rooms where a wall unit's throw pattern would leave corners underserved. Cassettes are a common choice for meeting rooms, reception areas, and mid-sized cabins where a visible wall unit would be aesthetically unwelcome but a full duct run would be overkill.

Cassettes still function as a single-zone unit like a split, one indoor unit paired to one outdoor condenser, or one indoor unit as part of a VRF branch, so the zoning logic is similar to split units. The real gain is airflow distribution and a cleaner ceiling-level appearance rather than a wall-mounted box.

Ducted AC: concealed, uniform, built for open floors

A ducted system conceals the indoor unit entirely above the false ceiling, distributing conditioned air through ductwork to multiple diffusers spread across a room or floor. This gives genuinely uniform cooling across an open-plan area without any visible indoor equipment, and a single indoor unit can serve several adjoining rooms through separate duct branches, useful for a cluster of small cabins that don't each need their own outdoor unit.

Ducted systems require more ceiling void depth than cassette or split units, and design quality matters enormously: poorly sized or leaky ductwork can quietly waste a large share of cooling capacity before it ever reaches a diffuser. Done well, though, ducted systems are the standard choice for large open floors, retail spaces, and any layout where visible indoor units aren't an option.

VRF systems: many zones, one efficient plant

VRF (Variable Refrigerant Flow, sometimes VRV) systems connect a large number of indoor units, a mix of cassettes, ducted units and wall splits, to a shared set of outdoor condensing units, with refrigerant flow to each indoor unit varying independently based on that zone's actual demand. A conference room running at full load and an unoccupied cabin idling nearby can be served from the very same outdoor plant, each getting exactly the refrigerant flow it needs at that moment.

The real advantage of VRF isn't any single indoor unit, it's what happens at the outdoor plant when dozens of zones are never all at peak load simultaneously.

This is what makes VRF the natural choice for larger, multi-zone buildings: fewer outdoor units overall relative to the number of zones served, strong part-load efficiency because compressors modulate to match aggregate demand rather than running at fixed capacity, and centralised control across the whole system. The trade-off is upfront cost and design complexity, VRF piping length and branch configuration need to be planned carefully with the building's core, shafts and rooftop space in mind, which means it rewards proper design far more than the other three options do.

Split or cassette almost always wins on cost and simplicity, VRF's advantages only show up at scale.

VRF's shared outdoor plant and independent zoning become genuinely cost-effective, not just convenient.

A simple decision framework

Rather than starting from a product preference, work through four questions in order. First, how many distinct zones does the space actually have, a zone being any area that should be controllable independently of the rest? A single-person office has one zone; a floor with a reception, four cabins, two meeting rooms and an open bullpen has at least seven. Second, what is the ceiling condition, is there void space for ducting, or is the ceiling height too tight to accommodate anything beyond a cassette or split? Third, how important is concealed aesthetics versus upfront budget, a client-facing reception area often justifies ducted or cassette even at a cost premium over a visible split unit. Fourth, how likely is the space to change, more zones or a full VRF backbone are easier to justify if you expect to reconfigure or expand within a few years.

Answering these four questions honestly, ideally alongside a proper heat-load assessment, narrows the choice far more reliably than comparing brochures or asking which brand is "best." In most real projects, the answer isn't even a single system type, it's a considered mix, such as VRF for the main floor with cassettes in meeting rooms and a split for an isolated server room.

Running costs and total cost of ownership

Purchase price tells only part of the story. What a system costs to run over its working life, typically ten to fifteen years for well-maintained equipment, depends heavily on how efficiently it operates at partial load, since most spaces spend the majority of their operating hours below peak occupancy, not at it. This is precisely where VRF's advantage compounds: its compressors modulate output to match aggregate zone demand, so a building running at sixty percent occupancy on an average day genuinely uses proportionally less energy, rather than several fixed-capacity split units each cycling on and off independently.

Ducted systems sit in between, with actual efficiency depending heavily on duct design and sealing quality, a well-designed ducted system can rival VRF's per-zone efficiency, while a poorly ducted one can quietly underperform its rated efficiency by a wide margin. Multiple standalone splits or cassettes tend to be least efficient at scale, simply because each compressor runs independently of how lightly loaded its neighbours are.

When comparing quotes, ask for running-cost estimates based on your actual usage pattern, operating hours, expected occupancy curve, and zone count, rather than relying on a single headline efficiency rating from a brochure. Factor in maintenance costs too: VRF systems generally need more specialised servicing than a simple split, so total cost of ownership should include AMC costs alongside the electricity bill, not just the electricity bill alone.

Key takeaways

  • Split and cassette suit single rooms; ducted suits open floors; VRF suits large, multi-zone buildings.
  • Zone count and ceiling void space usually narrow the choice faster than comparing brochures.
  • VRF's efficiency advantage comes from part-load performance across many zones, not any single indoor unit.
  • A well-designed ducted system can match VRF efficiency; a poorly ducted one can badly underperform.
  • Most real projects use a considered mix of system types rather than one uniform choice.
